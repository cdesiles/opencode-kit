# Brief

Ask once, with the `question` tool, then build. A round of focused questions is almost always right for a new project and almost never right for a follow-up.

## Always settle these

- **Audience and goal** — who reads it, and what they should do or believe afterwards.
- **Artifact and format** — deck, document, prototype, wireframe, poster, design system. The format determines everything downstream, so never infer it from a noun. "A page about our pricing" is not necessarily a web page.
- **Constraints** — brand, existing design system, must-keep copy, deadline-driven length.

If the answer is "you decide", decide and say which way you went in one line. Don't ask the same question twice.

## Ask when they change the output

- Length: slides, minutes, pages. For a deck, minutes matters more than slide count.
- Tone and register: formal boardroom, internal team, playful launch.
- References: screenshots, sites, files. A real reference beats any adjective.
- Interactivity: static or clickable, and what state it holds.
- Resolution of ambiguity: when two readings of the ask are plausible, ask which — it's cheaper than building both.

## Do not ask

- Anything the brief already answered.
- Permission to do the obvious part of the job.
- More than six questions at once. If you have more, you don't understand the task well enough to be asking — pick the ones that gate the build.
- "What colours do you like?" as a substitute for doing the work. Offer a direction and let them react.

## Output of this step

One short paragraph you can build from:

> A four-slide internal deck for the engineering all-hands, five minutes, plain and factual, no logo assets available, must include the three charts they pasted.

If you can't write that paragraph, ask another question.
